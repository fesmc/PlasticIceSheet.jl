Algorithms

This page describes how PlasticIceSheet.jl turns a margin, a bed and a basal shear stress into an ice sheet, how it differentiates the result with respect to the shear stress, and how the same discretization supports velocity, surface-mass-balance and SMB-driven reconstructions.

1. The governing equation

The reconstruction assumes perfectly plastic, steady-state ice. In that limit the basal shear stress balances the gravitational driving stress,

\tau_b = \tau_d = \rho_i\, g\, H\, |\nabla z_s| , \tag{1}

where the driving stress is set by the ice geometry. In a reconstruction \tau_b is prescribed (it is the field we choose or invert for) and the surface is the unknown. Rearranging for the surface slope gives the equation we solve:

|\nabla z_s| = \frac{k}{H} = \frac{k}{z_s - z_b}, \qquad k = \frac{\tau_b}{\rho_i\, g} . \tag{2}

Equation 2 is a static Hamilton–Jacobi equation. The surface is built inward from the margin, where H \to 0, and its upwind (viscosity) solution yields the correct envelope wherever ice from different margins meets. Ice divides, domes and merging ice caps therefore appear without any flowline or saddle handling. This is the physics of ICESHEET 2.0 (Gowan et al., 2016), solved on a grid rather than by tracing characteristics.

Three solver modes discretize Equation 2. :surface (the default) solves it for z_s directly (§2). :flat and :full solve it for w = H^2 (§3). The former is exact on a flat bed and the latter adds the bed gradient through an outer fixed point.

2. :surface mode

:surface solves Equation 2 for u = z_s in one fast-sweeping solve, with no outer iteration. Its Hamiltonian, |p| - k/(u - z_b), increases with u, since the right-hand side depends on z_s only through the thickness of the cell itself. The equation is therefore proper, and a monotone upwind scheme converges to its viscosity solution by fast sweeping (§5).

The discretization integrates the margin singularity. A cell-centred form, (u-a)_+^2/\Delta x^2 + \ldots = (k/(u - z_b))^2, is also monotone but inaccurate at the margin: on a flat bed its first ice cell is \sqrt{k\,\Delta x} thick instead of the Nye value \sqrt{2k\,\Delta x} (about 29 % low at every resolution). We therefore take each axis difference times the edge-mean thickness, with the neighbour thickness measured from the bed of the cell:

P_a(H) = \frac{(H - \alpha)_+ \,(H + \alpha_+)}{2}, \quad \alpha = a - z_b, \qquad \left(\frac{P_a(H)}{\Delta x}\right)^{2} + \left(\frac{P_b(H)}{\Delta y}\right)^{2} = k^2 , \tag{3}

where a and b are the upwind (smaller) neighbour surfaces along x and y, and \alpha_+ = \max(\alpha, 0). P_a increases with H and does not increase with a, so the scheme is proper and monotone. Where both neighbour surfaces stand above the bed of the cell, Equation 3 is the w-eikonal of §3 with w_a = \alpha^2 and has a closed-form root. Otherwise the root is found by Newton iteration from the smaller one-sided root, which decreases monotonically to the solution since P^2 is convex there. This is godunov_surface.

On a flat bed :surface is identical to :flat, including the exact 1-D Nye profile. Over bed relief it converges at first order: for a 1-D bed with slope 5\times10^{-3}, the maximum error against an ODE reference halves with each halving of \Delta x (about 1 m at \Delta x = 500 m), while :flat is off by about 300 m. On real beds at 32 km (GRL-32KM and NH-32KM) it converges in about 10 sweeps, while the :full fixed point does not converge for any relaxation tested (§3). These real-data results come from the Fortran port of the package, which uses the same scheme. For these reasons :surface is the default.

3. :flat and :full modes: solving for H^2

On a flat bed, multiplying Equation 2 by 2H and using \nabla(H^2) = 2H\nabla H gives a plain eikonal for w = H^2,

\bigl|\nabla w\bigr| = G, \qquad G = \frac{2\tau_b}{\rho_i\, g} = 2k , \tag{4}

with a finite right-hand side and the regular margin condition w = H_{flt}^2. :flat solves Equation 4 and drapes the thickness on the bed (z_s = z_b + \sqrt{w}). It is the cheapest mode and exact when bed relief is small compared with the ice thickness. Over relief, however, the surface follows the bed, which biases the thickness by a few hundred metres at 32 km on Greenland.

:full adds the bed gradient. Writing \nabla z_s = \nabla H + \nabla z_b in Equation 2 gives, exactly in one dimension,

|\nabla w| = \sqrt{\,G^2 - 4\sqrt{w}\,(\nabla z_b \cdot \nabla w) - 4\,w\,|\nabla z_b|^2\,} , \tag{5}

which reduces to Equation 4 on a flat bed. The right-hand side depends on \nabla w, so Equation 5 is solved by a damped (under-relaxed) Picard iteration: freeze the right-hand side from the current w, solve the eikonal for a candidate w_c, and update w \leftarrow (1-\alpha)\,w + \alpha\,w_c with \alpha = relax. The Hamiltonian of Equation 5 is increasing in w only where \nabla z_s \cdot \nabla z_b > 0, i.e., not on reverse bed slopes, and the lagged central-difference bed term makes the map non-contractive. On smooth synthetic relief the damped iteration converges. On GRL-32KM and NH-32KM, in contrast, it oscillates globally for every relaxation tested (0.4 to 0.02, 2000 iterations). :full is therefore kept for comparison with earlier results only.

4. The Godunov update

The eikonal |\nabla u| = F of :flat and :full is discretized cell by cell with the first-order Godunov upwind scheme. With a and b the smaller neighbour values along each axis,

a = \min(u_{i-1,j},\, u_{i+1,j}), \qquad b = \min(u_{i,j-1},\, u_{i,j+1}),

the update is the smallest u satisfying

\left(\frac{(u-a)_+}{\Delta x}\right)^{2} + \left(\frac{(u-b)_+}{\Delta y}\right)^{2} = F^2 .

The two-sided root is accepted if it exceeds both neighbours, and otherwise the smaller one-sided update \min(a + F\,\Delta x,\; b + F\,\Delta y) applies. This is godunov_eikonal. Both kernels are pure functions without mutation or indexing, which lets the AD back ends differentiate them directly.

Where an ice-free neighbour lies closer than a full spacing (a sub-cell margin, §6), the two sides of an axis have different spacings. The upwind term of that axis is then the maximum over the sides of (u - a_s)_+/d_s, and the update is the minimum over the side combinations. Each candidate is monotone, so the minimum is too.

5. Fast sweeping

The discrete equations are solved by the fast-sweeping method: Gauss–Seidel updates that keep the monotone minimum, u_{i,j} \leftarrow \min(u_{i,j},\ \text{update}), over the four diagonal traversal orderings in rotation. Each ordering follows the characteristics of one quadrant of directions, so a few sweeps converge the whole field in O(N) work. Ice cells start at +\infty and ice-free cells hold their boundary value (§6). An ice region with no ice-free cell is never reached; its cells are returned with H = 0 and a warning.

6. Boundary conditions and margin position

Ice-free cells carry the Dirichlet value of the margin. On land (z_b \ge z_{ss}) the margin thickness is zero. At a marine margin (z_b < z_{ss}) the grounding line sits at the flotation thickness,

H_{flt} = \frac{\rho_w}{\rho_i}\,\max(z_{ss} - z_b,\, 0),

which reproduces the marine treatment of ICESHEET. Sea level z_ss is an input like the bed and may be a field. The margin value is z_b + H_{flt} in :surface and H_{flt}^2 in :flat and :full.

The margin lies between an ice cell and its ice-free neighbour. The margin keyword sets where: the ice-free neighbour enters the update at (1 - f)\,\Delta x from the ice-cell centre, with f = 1/2 for :face (the default; margin on the cell face), f = 0 for :centre (margin on the ice-free cell centre, the behaviour before v0.2), or a field f \in [0, 1) on the ice-free cells. A margin closer than 1 % of a spacing to an ice-cell centre counts as at 1 %, which keeps the update well conditioned. For a margin given as a signed distance (positive in the ice), margin_frac_from_sdist returns f = -s_{dist}/\sqrt{\Delta x\,\Delta y} on the ice-free cells next to the ice. This value is isotropic and thus exact for margins along a grid axis.

The margin position matters at coarse resolution. For a circular cap of radius 30 km on a flat bed (centre off the grid), the volume error relative to the exact solution is:

\Delta x :centre :face signed distance
4 km +7.6 % −3.4 % −0.7 %
2 km +4.7 % −1.4 % −0.3 %
1 km +2.6 % −0.6 % −0.1 %
500 m +1.2 % −0.5 % −0.1 %

:centre places the margin a half cell too far out on average and overestimates the volume. :face has the opposite sign, since a staircase margin does not lie on average at the face. The signed distance is the most faithful margin when it is known. Against ICESHEET output for North America at 20 kyr ago and Greenland at present day, regridded from 5 km to 10–30 km, the margin position accounts for 0.4–5 percentage points of the volume excess of the coarse solution (Fortran port, same scheme).

7. Differentiating with respect to \tau_b

The solver is a pure function of \tau_b, so any objective on the output (a misfit to an observed surface, a target volume) is differentiable. This enables basal-shear-stress inversion (Inversion).

For a scalar \tau_b or a low-dimensional parameterization, ForwardDiff differentiates through solve. The cost scales with the number of parameters.

For a full per-cell field, the converged working field u (z_s or w) instead satisfies the Godunov residual

r_{i,j}(u, \tau_b) = u_{i,j} - \bigl(\text{Godunov update at } (i,j)\bigr) = 0

on ice cells (and u - \text{bc} = 0 elsewhere). By the implicit function theorem, for a loss L(u),

\frac{\partial L}{\partial \tau_b} = -\,\frac{\partial L}{\partial u}\left(\frac{\partial r}{\partial u}\right)^{-1}\frac{\partial r}{\partial \tau_b},

so the gradient costs one linear solve with the residual Jacobian, independent of the number of sweeps or outer iterations. Only the residual is differentiated (with ForwardDiff). This is wired up with ImplicitDifferentiation.jl in a package extension, so any reverse-mode AD (e.g., Zygote) obtains the gradient through differentiable_thickness. The :surface and :flat gradients agree with forward mode to machine precision. The :full gradient is exact only where the damped forward solve has driven the residual to zero.

NoteNaN hygiene

\sqrt{w} has an infinite derivative at w = 0. Wherever it is differentiated it is floored at H_{\min}^2, since the unguarded derivative produces \infty\cdot 0 = \text{NaN} in the reverse pass. :surface avoids the square root altogether.

8. Velocity and surface mass balance

The plastic solve returns geometry and nothing else. The perfectly plastic (n\to\infty) limit discards the rate-dependent information that a rate factor, a velocity and a surface mass balance (SMB) carry, so recovering them requires one extra closure on the fixed geometry.

The plastic balance Equation 1 is the membrane-free limit of the SSA/DIVA momentum balance: with \tau_d = \tau_b and the basal drag opposing flow, the divergence of the longitudinal stresses vanishes. The velocity is therefore local and analytical, and the ice flows down the surface gradient, \hat{\mathbf u} = -\nabla z_s/|\nabla z_s|. The geometry fixes the flow direction but not the flux magnitude. Closing that gap needs one assumption, which can be made in two dual ways.

Closure B: parameters → velocity → SMB

Assume a rheology and a sliding law. The depth-averaged speed is the sum of basal sliding and internal deformation, both at the local driving stress \tau = \tau_b:

|\bar{\mathbf u}| = u_b(\tau_b) + \frac{2A}{n+2}\,\tau_b^{\,n}\,H . \tag{6}

The deformational term is the SIA closed form with \tau_d = \tau_b, and u_b inverts the sliding law (basal_velocity). The sliding coefficient (\beta or C) may be a field. For a regularized-Coulomb bed at its cap the sliding speed is undetermined and is returned as NaN, unless a speed cap u_max is given. Steady-state mass conservation then gives the implied SMB as the flux divergence, \dot b = \nabla\cdot(\bar{\mathbf u}\,H) (diva_velocity, smb_from_velocity). The velocity is a smooth function of \tau_b, so the closure doubles as an observation operator for inversion.

Closure A: SMB → velocity

The dual prescribes \dot b and routes it down the fixed surface for the flux the ice must carry in steady state, the balance velocity (Budd and Warner, 1996): \nabla\cdot\mathbf q = \dot b, \mathbf q \parallel \hat{\mathbf u}, |\bar{\mathbf u}| = |\mathbf q|/H (balance_flux, balance_velocity).

Both closures use one finite-volume multiple-flow-direction (MFD) operator, which partitions the throughput of a cell to its lower ice neighbours in proportion to their surface drop. Routing and the implied-SMB divergence use the same weights, so balance_flux(smb_from_velocity(u)) returns \bar{\mathbf u} H to machine precision. Subtracting the deformation Equation 6 from the balance speed leaves the basal velocity that geometry and SMB jointly demand (implied_basal_velocity). This is a check on whether the plastic geometry and an assumed climate are consistent with a plausible bed.

9. SMB-driven reconstruction

The closures also support the dual of solve: reconstruct the geometry driven by an SMB instead of a yield stress, on the same prescribed margin (diva_reconstruct). Two methods are available. :steady (the default) solves steady mass conservation directly. :routing derives \tau_b by routing and is only conditionally stable.

:steady: steady mass conservation

The unknown is H \ge 0 on the ice cells, with the membrane-free DIVA flux of Equation 6 and the driving stress taken from the geometry:

\mathbf q = -H\,U(\tau, H)\,\frac{\nabla z_s}{|\nabla z_s|}, \qquad \tau = \rho_i g H |\nabla z_s|, \qquad U = u_b(\tau) + \frac{2A}{n+2}\tau^n H .

With the obstacle H \ge 0, steady state is the complementarity problem

H \ge 0, \qquad F(H) = \nabla\cdot\mathbf q - \dot b \ge 0, \qquad H\,F(H) = 0 ,

where F > 0 at H = 0 marks a cell where ablation exceeds the inflow. This is the steady SIA with sliding, a monotone degenerate-elliptic problem with a unique solution.

Fluxes are staggered on the cell faces. Between two ice cells the face thickness is the mean where the surface drop \Delta s across the face is small against the upstream thickness, and limited towards the upstream value where it is not (downstream weight \min(1/2,\ H_{up}/(4\Delta s))). This keeps the face flux non-increasing in the downstream thickness, so F is non-increasing in the neighbouring thicknesses, as the active-set Newton step requires. The tangential slope is the mean of the central differences at the two cells. Between an ice cell and an ice-free neighbour the flux is the one-sided SIA flux to the margin, which lies at the margin position of §6. It uses the slope and the mean thickness of the segment between cell centre and margin, and never flows inward. Unlike the plastic profile, whose flux tends to zero at a land margin, this flux grows with the thickness of the ice cell, so every SMB has a steady state. For a Coulomb bed without a speed cap, the driving stress of the fluxes is capped at 0.999\,C.

The implied SMB of a geometry is the divergence of the same face fluxes (smb_flux_divergence). A geometry forced by its own implied SMB is therefore a root of F. smb_flux_divergence also returns the outflow through the margin faces of each ice cell, split into land and marine (an ice-free neighbour below sea level), and the surface velocity of the same closure. An optional τ_cap limits the driving stress of the fluxes, e.g., to \tau_b, since a plastic bed carries at most its yield stress.

The solver is pseudo-transient continuation with one semismooth Newton step per pseudo-time step. Each step solves \Phi(H) = \min\!\bigl(H,\ c\,G(H)\bigr) = 0 for G(H) = (H - H_k)/\Delta t + F(H), where a cell is active (H = 0) when its own Newton step would empty it. The Jacobian \partial F/\partial H is computed by finite differences with nine colours, since F at a cell depends only on the 3\times3 block around it. The linear system over the inactive cells is solved directly (SparseArrays). The new iterate is \max(H + \lambda\,\delta H, 0), with \lambda halved until the rms residual decreases. The pseudo-time step grows by switched evolution relaxation (at least 1.5× after a step that does not increase the residual), and the iteration stops when \max|\min(H, F)| falls below res_tol. The pseudo-time term keeps the system well posed at divides, where \partial F/\partial H vanishes with the surface slope.

In tests the method recovers the plastic geometry from a cold start under its own implied SMB to about 10^{-12} m in a few tens of steps. For the Vialov profile (n = 3, \dot b = 0.3 m yr⁻¹, L = 300 km) the maximum relative error is about 2.6, 1.1, 0.6 and 0.3 % at \Delta x = 20, 10, 5 and 2.5 km, i.e., first order.

:routing: derived yield stress

:routing iterates the plastic solve with \tau_b derived from the SMB: (1) route \dot b down the current surface for the throughput (§8), (2) invert the flux law Equation 6 per cell for the driving stress that carries it (bisection), (3) solve the plastic equation with that stress (inner_mode), and (4) under-relax and repeat. A plastic solution forced by its own closure-B SMB is an exact fixed point of this map.

The map is, however, only conditionally stable. The MFD weights split the flux of a cell by surface drops of slope × \Delta x, i.e., a few metres, so a thickness change \delta H moves the split, the throughput and the derived stress by O(\delta H/(\text{slope}\,\Delta x)). The Jacobian of the map at the fixed point thus has eigenvalues \lambda \sim -H/(\text{slope}\,\Delta x), about −50 to −600 in our tests, and the under-relaxed iteration is stable only for relax < 2/(1-\lambda), i.e., 0.003–0.04. The bound shrinks with resolution, and routing changes make the map discontinuous. This is a structural property of the method rather than a tuning problem, which is why :steady is the default.

10. What was deliberately dropped

Relative to ICESHEET 2.0, the grid Hamilton–Jacobi solver makes the following machinery unnecessary: method-of-characteristics flowline tracing, crossover detection (the motorcycle-graph algorithm), saddle and dome detection with polygon splitting, the GIA and sea-level iteration loop, and the GMT projection and preprocessing pipeline.
